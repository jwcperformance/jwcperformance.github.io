What a Good Service Page Looks Like: A Simple Layout for Trades

Your service pages do more work than any other page on your website. They're what Google ranks, where your ads should land, and what AI tools read to work out what you do. Here's a simple top to bottom layout that works for almost any trade, plus the mistakes that hold most service pages back.

Why service pages matter so much

Most people don't search for your business name. They search for the job. "Boiler repair Bromley." "Flat roof replacement Croydon." The page that shows up for that search is almost always a service page, not your homepage.

A good service page does three jobs at once:

  • It helps you rank. One clear page per service gives Google something specific to show for each search.
  • It turns visitors into enquiries. It's where people decide whether to call you or the next business.
  • It feeds ads and AI. Your Google Ads should land on it, and AI tools read it to understand what you offer. I explain why that matters in your website is half your Google Ads result.

Google has also been clear about what it values. In October 2026 it updated its guidance on helpful content to put more weight on the quality of a page's main content, and on the effort and originality behind it. A thin page with a few lines of copy and a stock photo doesn't tick many of those boxes.

The layout, top to bottom

1. Service and area in the heading

Your main heading should say exactly what the page is about. "Boiler repairs in Bromley" is far better than "Our services" or "Welcome". Underneath, add one plain line on who you help and why they'd pick you. Then a call button, visible straight away on a phone.

2. What's included and how it works

Explain the job in plain words. What happens when someone calls? Do you visit first? How long does it take? What's included, and what isn't? This is the part most trade websites skip, and it's what makes people feel safe picking up the phone.

3. Prices or price ranges

You don't need a full price list. But "boiler repairs from £85" or "most jobs fall between £X and £Y" tells people you're open and helps them judge if you're right for them. It also means fewer time wasting calls.

4. Real proof for this job

Show photos of this type of work, not just any job. Add a couple of reviews that mention this service. Show the accreditations that matter for it, like Gas Safe for boilers or NICEIC for electrics. Proof works best near your call button.

5. Areas you cover

List the towns you actually work in, by name. "We cover Bromley, Beckenham, Orpington and Chislehurst" is clearer for people and for Google than "within 20 miles of our base".

6. Honest FAQs

Use the questions people really ask you on the phone. Do you charge a call out fee? Do you work weekends? How quickly can you come out? Answering them on the page saves you time and gives AI tools clear answers to quote. My post on what Google's AI is saying about your business explains why that matters.

7. A second chance to get in touch

By the time someone scrolls to the bottom, they're ready. Give them a short form and your phone number again. Make sure both are tracked, so you know which pages bring in work.

8. Links to related services

Someone reading about boiler repairs might need a new boiler instead. Link to your related pages. It helps visitors, and it helps Google understand how your services fit together.

How long should a service page be? Long enough to answer the questions above, and no longer. For most trades that ends up somewhere between 500 and 1,000 words. Don't pad it to hit a number. Every section should help someone decide to call.

Common mistakes that hold service pages back

  • One page for everything. A single "Services" page with a list can't rank for each job. Give each main service its own page.
  • Copy and paste town pages. Ten pages with the same text and a different town name swapped in look thin to Google and to customers. Only build area pages where people really search, and make each one genuinely useful. My post on local SEO for multi area businesses covers how.
  • Stock photos. Customers can tell. One real photo of your work beats a gallery of strangers in hard hats.
  • No prices at all. People often leave to find someone who gives them a rough idea.
  • The phone number hidden at the top. On a phone, it should be one tap away wherever someone is on the page.
  • Text inside images. Google and AI tools can't read words baked into a banner. My post on whether your website is invisible to AI search shows how to check.

A quick example

Here's how a boiler repair page for a heating engineer in Bromley might run:

  1. Heading: Boiler repairs in Bromley. Same week appointments from a Gas Safe engineer. Call button.
  2. How it works: Call or book online, we diagnose the fault, give a fixed quote before any work, and fix most faults on the first visit.
  3. Prices: Diagnostic visit from £X. Most repairs between £X and £Y.
  4. Proof: Three photos of recent repairs, two reviews mentioning boiler repairs, Gas Safe logo.
  5. Areas: Bromley, Beckenham, Orpington, Chislehurst, Hayes and West Wickham.
  6. FAQs: Call out fee, weekend work, which brands you repair, what happens if the part needs ordering.
  7. Enquire: Short form and phone number.
  8. Related: New boiler installs, boiler servicing, power flushing.

Nothing fancy. Just clear, specific and useful. That's what wins both the ranking and the call. For the rest of the website checklist, see what a trade website needs in 2026.

Common questions

Should every service have its own page?

Every main service should. If you'd want to rank for it or run ads to it, it needs its own page. Very small extras can sit on a related service page.

How long should a service page be?

Long enough to explain the job, prices, proof, areas and common questions. For most trades that's somewhere between 500 and 1,000 words, but usefulness matters more than word count.

Should I put prices on my service pages?

Usually, yes. A starting price or a typical range builds trust, filters out people who aren't a fit, and gives AI tools a clear answer when someone asks about cost.

Do I need a separate page for each town?

Only where people actually search for your service in that town, and only if each page is genuinely useful. Copied pages with the town name swapped in can do more harm than good.

The bottom line

A good service page says what you do and where in the heading, explains the job, gives a rough price, shows real proof, lists your areas, answers honest questions and makes calling easy. Do that for each main service and you've built the pages that Google ranks, your ads land on and AI tools read.
